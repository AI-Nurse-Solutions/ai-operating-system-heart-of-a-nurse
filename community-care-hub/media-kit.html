About 50 words

The Community Care Hub is a free, open-source system designed by nurses for community networks. AI helpers handle the busywork: welcoming new members, keeping resource lists current, sending reminders, and drafting impact reports. People make every decision. The community owns the hub, its data, and its choice of AI. Created by the Nurse Intelligence Network.

About 150 words

At 3 a.m. in the ICU, families ask nurses the same question: who do I call when we get home? The help usually exists. It's just scattered. The Community Care Hub is a nurse-designed system that helps a community connect those pieces on purpose. A local network, such as a clinic, faith community, veterans' group, or nurse coalition, sets up a hub on a computer it controls and chooses the AI that powers it. AI helpers welcome new members, maintain a verified list of local services, coordinate events, and draft a monthly impact brief. A named person approves everything that goes out, and anyone in crisis is sent straight to a person. The hub is free and open source under the MIT License, maps its safeguards to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and stays with the community no matter who holds office. It was created by the Nurse Intelligence Network, LLC, and is planned to move to the NAIO Institute, a planned nurse-led nonprofit.

The concept

The problem

Communities have energy but no system to catch it. Forty people sign up at a town hall, and two weeks later six are still involved. Families can't find help that already exists. Small groups can't prove their impact.

The idea

Apply the way nurses coordinate care under pressure to community organizing. A hub that welcomes, coordinates, connects, and reports, while people keep every decision.

The model

Free setup for one local network, a 90-day pilot, then it belongs to them. Public offices can refer a group without buying, hosting, or running anything.

How a hub is built: a group assesses its community, names one need, and writes its mission using the nursing process (Assess, Diagnose, Plan, Implement, Evaluate). Then it chooses only the AI helpers its findings call for. See the Cause Starter Packs and the Hub Setup Guide.

Why it matters

Nurses are stepping up to set the rules for AI

Registered nurses are the largest healthcare occupation in the U.S., about 3.4 million employed in 2022 (BLS). On May 5, 2026, the American Nurses Association said AI in nursing "requires deliberate, nurse-led action to protect patient safety, ensure nurse well-being, and sustain public trust" (ANA). This ecosystem puts nurse-led guardrails into working tools.

From the bedside to the block

Nursing's method of assessing, diagnosing, planning, acting, and evaluating is applied to community organizing. Nurses already coordinate families, specialists, and community resources every day. The hub hands that discipline to neighborhood leaders.

Communities own their tools

The hub is open source, can run on a group's own computer, and works with the AI the group chooses. There's no vendor to wait on and no price that can change underneath them. Its safeguards map to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework as a voluntary self-assessment.

Safety designed in, not added on

No AI counseling, no crisis response, and no private health records. A named person approves every outgoing message, and anyone in crisis is directed to people and to 988.

A nonpartisan bridge to public service

Elected offices can refer a trusted local group without buying or running anything, and because the community owns the hub, it doesn't disappear when an office changes hands.

Where things stand: the Community Care Hub launched in November 2026, and the first community pilots are forming now. Results will be shared after the first 90-day pilots, not before.

Founder

Short bio

Robert Domondon, RN, CCRN, CSC, CMC, is an ICU nurse with more than twenty years of night shifts and the founder of the Nurse Intelligence Network. He builds nurse-led, open-source AI tools that keep people in charge of the decisions that matter.

Longer bio

Robert Domondon, RN, CCRN, CSC, CMC, has worked night shifts in intensive care for more than twenty years. Before his years at the bedside, he trained as a physician in the Philippines and served as hospital administrator of a former U.S. Naval Hospital. He founded the Nurse Intelligence Network to help nurses lead how AI enters health and community care, and is organizing the NAIO Institute, a planned nurse-led nonprofit for AI standards. His work rests on one conviction: the people who coordinate care under pressure should be the ones who coordinate AI under governance.
