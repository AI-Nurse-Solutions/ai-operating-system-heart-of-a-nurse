Technology Stack and Sovereignty Levels

Sovereignty means your community controls its own tools, data, and power. It isn't all or nothing. This page defines five levels, walks through the stack layer by layer, and helps you choose the level that fits your group. Most groups should start at Level 2 or 3 and climb only when they need to.

The five levels of sovereignty

Think of it like levels of care: you match the level to the need, and you can step up or down.

1
Cloud-convenient

Everything runs on other companies' services: the AI comes bundled with a paid Hermes plan. Easiest to start. You depend on those companies for privacy, price, and uptime.

Fits: public information only, a group just trying it out.
2
Own-account cloud

The hub runs on your computer and your files stay there, but the AI is a cloud service under your own account (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or others). You control the account and can switch providers. Your prompts still travel to that provider.

Fits: most new hubs with low-sensitivity information.
3
Hybrid

A local AI on your own computer handles anything sensitive, like member names and contact details. Cloud AI handles public-facing work: research, drafting newsletters, social posts. You decide which is which, and write it down.

Fits: groups holding member lists; the best balance for most.
4
Local-first

All AI runs on hardware you own, or on a private GPU server you rent and control. The internet is used only to send and receive messages. Nothing is processed by an AI company.

Fits: groups with strong privacy needs and a technology volunteer.
5
Self-sustaining sovereign

Level 4, plus your own power: battery or solar backup, offline backups you can carry, and printed essentials. The hub keeps working through grid and internet outages.

Fits: disaster-prone areas, resilience hubs, groups that must not go dark.

Honest trade-off: each step up gives more control and privacy, and asks more of you: more setup skill, more hardware, and more upkeep. Local AI models are also less capable than the largest cloud models on complex tasks. Higher isn't better. Fitting is better.

A small AI model on a laptop draws little power. A desktop with a large graphics card draws several times more under load. Choosing a model sized to the job is the biggest energy decision you'll make.

Layer 2 · The computer, and containerized or not

Hardware: any recent Windows, Mac, or Linux computer runs the hub at Levels 1 and 2. Running a local AI (Levels 3 to 5) needs more memory. A dedicated computer for the hub is ideal. If you share one, create a separate login for the hub so personal files and community files never mix.

SetupWhat it meansBest for
Plain installInstall the app like any other program.Most groups. Simplest to set up and maintain.
ContainerizedThe hub runs inside a sealed "box" (such as Docker) that separates it from the rest of the computer.Groups with a technology volunteer. Easier to move, copy to other sites, and restore. Limits what the helpers can touch.
Dedicated machine or virtual machineThe hub has a whole computer, real or virtual, to itself.The strongest separation. Good for Levels 4 and 5.

Container support and options change. Have your advisor check the official Hermes documentation for current guidance.

Layer 3 · Data and backups

  • Where data lives: on the hub computer at every level. At Levels 1 to 3, some text also travels to a cloud AI when a helper works on it.
  • Encryption: turn on full-disk encryption (built into Windows, Mac, and Linux) so a stolen computer doesn't expose your members.
  • Backups, the 3-2-1 rule: three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one kept somewhere else. For example: the hub computer, an encrypted external drive, and an encrypted copy at a leader's home or in a cloud folder you control.
  • Offline essentials (Level 5): a printed resource list and phone tree, updated monthly.
  • Test it: restore from backup once a month. An untested backup is only a hope.

Choosing a model

  • Size to the job. Drafting a welcome note needs a small model. Long research summaries need a larger one. Start small.
  • Check it can use tools. Helpers need a model that reliably follows instructions and uses tools. Not every small model does this well. Test before you rely on it.
  • Match your hardware. As a rough guide: 8 GB of memory runs only the smallest models; 16 GB runs small, useful ones; 32 GB or a dedicated graphics card runs mid-sized models. Apple computers with M-series chips are efficient at this.
  • Check the license. Open models come with different licenses. Confirm yours allows organizational use.
  • Check your languages. If your community speaks Spanish, Tagalog, or Hmong, test the model in those languages.
  • Be honest about limits. Local models make more mistakes on complex tasks than the largest cloud models. Human approval matters even more.

If you rent a GPU

  • Choose a dedicated machine from an established provider, not shared or peer-hosted hardware.
  • Ask for their security attestations (such as SOC 2), and choose a U.S. region if location matters to you.
  • Encrypt the disk, keep access keys private, and delete the machine and its storage when the job is done.
  • Never send health records or ID numbers, the same rule as everywhere else in the hub.

Layers 5 and 6 · Agent software and the application layer

These stay the same at every level. That's the point: you can change what's underneath without rebuilding your hub.

  • Agent software: Hermes Desktop, open source, from Nous Research.
  • Soul file: your mission, voice, and ground rules, in plain text you can read and edit.
  • Helpers: Member Welcome, Resource Navigator, Event Coordinator, Impact Reporter, and any you add.
  • Approval rules: green, yellow, red. A named person approves anything that goes out.
  • Scheduled jobs: backups, the daily summary, weekly safety checks. They report and draft only.

Because the soul file and helper definitions are plain files, moving from Level 2 to Level 4 means changing the AI setting, not starting over.

Layer 7 · Front-facing services: research, email, marketing, communications

Some work has to touch the outside world. Sovereignty here means you own the master copy and can leave any service.

ServiceWhy cloud makes senseHow to keep control
Research and web searchThe information is public and lives online.Never put member names or private details in a search or research request.
EmailEmail travels through other companies' servers by nature.Use an organizational address, ideally on a domain you own, so you can change providers without losing your address.
Newsletters and marketingSending tools handle delivery, unsubscribes, and legal requirements.Keep the master member list in the hub. Upload only what's needed to send. Export regularly.
Social mediaThat's where your community already is.Treat posts as public. Keep originals in the hub. A person approves every post.
Forms and sign-upsEasy for the public to reach.Ask only for what you need. Move responses into the hub and clear the form tool regularly.
Phone and textThe most reliable way to reach people in a crisis.Keep a printed phone tree. It works when nothing else does.

Cloud AI is a reasonable choice for drafting public content, like a flyer or a social post, even at Level 3. The content is going public anyway.

Which level fits you?

What's the most sensitive thing your hub will hold?
Public information only

Events, resource lists, announcements.

Start at Level 1 or 2.

Member names and contacts

Sign-ups, volunteer rosters, phone numbers.

Level 3, hybrid.

Must not go dark

Disaster-prone area, or a group people rely on in emergencies.

Work toward Level 4 or 5.

Health records, ID numbers, and financial details don't belong in the hub at any level.

A sensible path: start at Level 2 to learn the hub. Move to Level 3 when you add member lists. Add backup power when your first outage teaches you why. Write your level in your Charter and review it at each 90-day evaluation.
