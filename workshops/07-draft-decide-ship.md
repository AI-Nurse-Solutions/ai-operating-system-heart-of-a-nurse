# 07 — Draft, Decide, Ship

### Finish Real Work in One Sitting

**Format:** 60 minutes · live or virtual · bring one unfinished thing that has a real deadline
**Series:** Nurse AI OS — General Practice Workshops
**Boundary:** Non-clinical. No patient care, no medical advice, no PHI. Bring a proposal, an
application, a page, a letter, a policy for your own business, a talk, a pitch.

---

## The promise

> **Setup:** The reason that thing still isn't done isn't that you're lazy, and it isn't that
> you're busy. You've finished harder things than that while busier than this.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> **Reveal:** It's that you've been trying to start and finish in the same motion — and there is
> now a tool that is spectacular at exactly one of those two things, and useless at the other.

**One sentence:** You will take one real unfinished piece of work from blank to
good-enough-to-send inside this hour, and leave with the three-pass method that makes that
repeatable.

---

## Who this is for

Professionals with a proposal, report, or application sitting at 40% for three weeks.
Founders who need a page, a pitch, or a policy and keep re-outlining it. Nurses writing
non-clinical work — a course, an article, a business plan, a grant application, a conference
abstract. Small-business owners who write everything themselves and therefore write nothing.
Anyone whose bottleneck is finishing rather than knowing.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Separate **drafting from deciding**, and explain why collapsing the two is what produces the
   blank page.
2. Write a **brief** — audience, outcome, constraints, voice, done-condition, exclusions —
   good enough that someone else could draft from it.
3. Run the **three-pass method**: draft wide · cut hard · check honestly.
4. Apply the **30% cut rule** and defend what survives.
5. Run a **critique pass** that surfaces vagueness, unsupported claims, and the objection a
   skeptical reader would actually raise.
6. Recognise and remove the **tells** — the tonal fingerprints that make writing read as
   machine-made — and put their own voice back.
7. Hold the **human gate**: nothing goes out without a person deciding it goes out.

## Run of show (60 minutes)

| Time | Segment | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | **Open: name the thing** | Write it on a card and put it face up where you can see it. Say the deadline out loud to your neighbour. It ships before this hour ends. |
| 5–11 | **Teach: the bottleneck moved** | Producing text used to be the hard part; now evaluating it is. Your job changed from writer to editor-in-chief, and nobody sent the memo. What an editor-in-chief actually does. |
| 11–17 | **Write the brief** | Six minutes: who it's for · what you want them to do · constraints · voice · what "done" means · what must not appear. Most people have never written this down for their own work, and it is why the drafts keep failing. |
| 17–22 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 1 — Brief in, honest first draft out** | *See notes below.* |
| 22–34 | **Work: draft, then cut** | Draft from the brief. Then cut 30% — measured, not estimated. What survives a 30% cut is nearly always better than what you'd have written carefully. |
| 34–39 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 2 — The critique pass** | *See notes below.* |
| 39–46 | **Voice pass** | Read one paragraph out loud. If you would not say it to a person, rewrite it. Strike the tells: the tricolon, the "not just X but Y", the hedged summary paragraph that says nothing. |
| 46–51 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 3 — The gate** | *See notes below.* |
| 51–56 | **Ship** | Send it. Post it. Submit it. In the room, while everyone watches. This segment is non-negotiable and it is why people come back. |
| 56–60 | **Close** | Reflection and call to action. |

## Demo pause notes

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 1 — Brief in, honest first draft out (≈5 min).**
Put a real brief in and show the first draft — including the parts that are bad. Do not
cherry-pick. Then show the second pass driven by one *specific* critique rather than "make it
better". The lesson is that the quality of the second draft is entirely a function of the
quality of the complaint.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 2 — The critique pass (≈5 min).**
Ask the workspace: *where is this vague · what would a skeptical reader push back on · what did
I claim without support · what did I leave out that the brief asked for.* Read the critique
aloud. It will be uncomfortable and correct. That discomfort is the product.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 3 — The gate (≈5 min).**
Show the final step: a human approving before anything is sent, published, or spent. Say it
directly — the value of a system that drafts brilliantly is entirely dependent on it never
being allowed to send.

---

## Practical takeaways (usable tonight)

- **The brief template** — six fields, one page, reusable for everything they write.
- **The three-pass method:** draft wide · cut 30% · critique honestly.
- **Three critique prompts** written out verbatim.
- **The tells checklist** for stripping machine-voice out of their own writing.
- **One finished, shipped piece of work.** Not a plan to finish it. Finished.

---

## Closing reflection and call to action

> The work that changes your situation is almost never the difficult work you were afraid of.
> It's the ordinary work you never finished — the application, the follow-up, the page, the
> proposal that sat at 40% until the moment passed.
>
> Nobody is ever going to see the draft. They only ever see whether it arrived.

**Call to action:** Put this hour in your calendar as a weekly appointment. Same hour, same
method, one unfinished thing each time. Fifty-two finished things a year will change your life
more than any tool ever will.
