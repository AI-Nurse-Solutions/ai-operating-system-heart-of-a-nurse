# 08 — The Decision Desk

### Making Better Calls With Less Regret

**Format:** 60 minutes · live or virtual · bring one live decision you're actually carrying
**Series:** Nurse AI OS — General Practice Workshops
**Boundary:** Non-clinical. No patient care, no medical advice, no PHI, no clinical decisions
of any kind. Bring a career, business, money, time, or life decision.

---

## The promise

> **Setup:** You are not short of information about this decision. You have read more than you
> needed to. You have asked three people. You have a folder.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> **Reveal:** You're short of somewhere to put it — and short of a record of how you decided last
> time, which is the only thing that has ever made anyone wiser.

**One sentence:** You will build a decision desk — a repeatable, one-page way to frame a real
decision, stress-test it against the strongest case for the opposite, and record it so your
future self can actually learn from it.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone standing at a genuine fork: a job, a move, a hire, a price, a partnership, a course, a
loan, a no. Leaders who decide for other people and want a defensible method. Founders making
irreversible calls with incomplete information — which is all of them. Nurses making career and
business decisions where the stakes are personal rather than clinical. Anyone who has made the
same category of mistake twice.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. **Frame** a decision as a real question with real options, and recognise that most bad
   decisions were badly framed rather than badly reasoned.
2. Classify a decision as **reversible or irreversible**, and set decision speed accordingly —
   fast for reversible, slow and documented for the rest.
3. Generate at least three genuine options, one of which is always **do nothing**.
4. Construct the **strongest case against** their own preference, and take it seriously.
5. Run a **pre-mortem**: it is a year from now and this went badly — write the story.
6. Name the **assumptions** underneath their preference and the specific evidence that would
   break each one.
7. Write a **decision record** with a review date, and reopen an old one without flinching.

## Run of show (60 minutes)

| Time | Segment | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | **Open: name it in one line** | Write the decision you're carrying as a single question. Most people write a *situation*, not a question. That gap is the first lesson and it lands before you've taught anything. |
| 5–12 | **Teach: framing and reversibility** | Why "should I take the job?" is usually the wrong question. The reversibility test and what it licenses: reversible decisions should be made quickly and badly-ish; irreversible ones deserve the full desk. |
| 12–17 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 1 — Reframing a vague dilemma** | *See notes below.* |
| 17–26 | **Exercise: the one-pager** | Question · at least three options including *do nothing* · what actually matters to me here (ranked) · what I would need to believe for each option to be right. Nine minutes, silent. |
| 26–31 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 2 — Steelman and pre-mortem** | *See notes below.* |
| 31–39 | **Disconfirmation** | Write the three assumptions your preferred option rests on. Beside each, the evidence that would break it — specific, findable evidence, not "if things changed". Then note whether you could go and look for it this week. |
| 39–44 | **⏸ DEMO PAUSE 3 — The record and the ledger** | *See notes below.* |
| 44–53 | **Pairs: what would have to be true** | Read your one-pager aloud. Your partner has exactly one job and one question, asked repeatedly: *what would have to be true for that to be right?* No advice permitted. The ban on advice is what makes this work. |
| 53–60 | **Close** | Reflection, call to action, and setting review dates. |

## Demo pause notes

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 1 — Reframing a vague dilemma (≈5 min).**
Give the workspace a genuinely woolly dilemma and ask it not for an answer but for the real
question underneath, plus the options you haven't considered. Show the options list. There is
almost always one obvious option nobody in the room had written down.
*Do not show:* a decision involving another identifiable person's private circumstances.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 2 — Steelman and pre-mortem (≈5 min).**
Two prompts, back to back. First: make the strongest possible case for the option I like
least. Second: it is a year from now, I chose my preferred option, and it went badly — write
the story of how. Read the pre-mortem aloud. Rooms go quiet, because the story is usually
recognisable.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 3 — The record and the ledger (≈5 min).**
Write the decision record live: the question, the options, what was chosen, why, what would
change it, and the review date. Then open one from months ago and read it, including the part
that turned out to be wrong. Modelling that this is survivable is the actual demonstration.

---

## Practical takeaways (usable tonight)

- **The decision one-pager**, completed for a decision they are genuinely carrying.
- **The reversibility test**, which will speed up half their decisions immediately.
- **The pre-mortem prompt**, written out verbatim.
- **A disconfirmation list**: three assumptions, three breakers, one thing to go and check
  this week.
- **The decision record format** plus a review date already in the calendar.

---

## Closing reflection and call to action

> Good judgment comes from experience. Experience comes from bad judgment.
>
> But only if you wrote it down. Otherwise experience is just a feeling of having been here
> before, attached to no evidence about what you actually thought at the time — and memory
> will quietly rewrite you into someone who saw it coming.

**Call to action:** Record your next three decisions, however small, in the one-page format.
Put a review date on each. In ninety days, open all three and read them honestly. That
half-hour will teach you more about how you think than any book about thinking.
