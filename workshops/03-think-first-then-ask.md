# 03 — Think First, Then Ask

### How to Use AI Every Day Without Losing Your Own Mind

**Format:** 60 minutes · live or virtual · devices closed for the first exercise
**Series:** Nurse AI OS — General Practice Workshops
**Boundary:** Non-clinical. No patient care, no medical advice, no PHI. All practice problems
are the participant's own, from work or life outside clinical care.

---

## The promise

> **Setup:** The danger was never that AI gives you a wrong answer. You can catch a wrong
> answer.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> **Reveal:** The danger is that it gives you a *good* answer, instantly, before you ever found
> out what you thought. Do that a few hundred times and you stop having a first thought at
> all.

**One sentence:** You will practice commit-then-compare — the single discipline that lets you
use AI daily and get sharper instead of duller — on a real problem you brought with you.

---

## Who this is for

Daily users who've noticed they reach for it before they think. Managers and professionals
whose judgment is the actual product they sell. Students worried they're learning the tool
instead of the subject. Parents and mentors trying to explain to someone younger why "just
ask it" isn't a method. Anyone who has defended an AI answer in a meeting and then privately
realised they couldn't have.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

1. Explain why **fluency reads as correctness**, and describe what a language model is
   actually doing when it sounds certain.
2. Run **commit-then-compare** on a real question: write your own answer first, then consult,
   then diff.
3. Write **disconfirming prompts** that ask the system to argue against the position it just
   handed you.
4. Recognise the three early signals of **skill atrophy** in themselves, before it costs them
   something.
5. Use the **Ask Ladder** to decide, in the moment, whether a question belongs to their own
   head, a person, the AI, or nobody.
6. Design a personal **unassisted check** — a recurring task done with no help, to prove the
   underlying capability is still there.

## Demo pause notes

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 1 — Confident wrongness (≈4 min).**
Ask for something specific and checkable — a figure, a citation, a date, a quotation — and
then check it live in front of the room. Do not rig it. If it comes back correct, say so and
check a second one. The lesson survives either outcome: the point is that *you had to check*,
and that nothing in the tone told you which kind of answer you got.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 2 — The disconfirming pass (≈5 min).**
Take an answer the workspace just produced and ask it to make the strongest possible case
that the answer is wrong, and to state what would have to be true for the objection to hold.
Read both aloud. The room will notice it argues the second position just as fluently — which
is exactly the point about fluency.

**⏸ DEMO PAUSE 3 — Mark what you can't verify (≈5 min).**
Ask the workspace to go back through its own answer and flag every claim it cannot support
from a source it can name. Show the flags. This is the habit that turns an answer into a
draft, and a draft is something you can safely use.

---

## Practical takeaways (usable tonight)

- **The commit-then-compare card:** answer first, consult second, diff third. Four minutes.
- **Three disconfirming prompts** written out verbatim, ready to paste: *argue against this ·
  what would have to be true · mark what you cannot verify.*
- **The Ask Ladder**, on one page, for the moment when the reflex to reach for the box arrives.
- **The atrophy checklist** — three signals: you can't produce a first draft cold; you can't
  explain the answer you're defending; you feel anxious starting without it.
- **One monthly unassisted check**, on the calendar before they leave.

---

## Closing reflection and call to action

> The calculator did not ruin arithmetic for the people who already understood arithmetic. It
> ruined it for the ones who never got there — and they didn't notice, because the answers on
> the screen looked exactly the same.
>
> You cannot tell, from the output, which of those two people you are becoming. The only way
> to know is to occasionally put the thing down and find out.

**Call to action:** The next real decision you face — this week, not hypothetically — write
your own answer first. On paper. Before you ask. Then compare, and keep the diff. That diff
is the most honest record of your own thinking you will ever get.
