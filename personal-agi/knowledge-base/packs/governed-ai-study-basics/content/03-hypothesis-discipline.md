# Hypothesis discipline

Every AI output is a **hypothesis** — a plausible draft of an answer, not a verdict. The discipline is refusing to let fluency stand in for evidence, no matter how confident the prose sounds.

## The practice

1. **Ask where it came from.** An answer you cannot trace is an answer you cannot trust. Ask the AI for its sources, then check that they exist and say what the AI claims they say — fabricated citations remain a routine failure mode.
2. **Verify against an authority the AI does not control.** Your assigned text, your instructor, the practice standard, the actual policy document. AI summarizing a source is not the source.
3. **Prefer tools that abstain.** A system that says "I don't have a grounded answer" is showing you the behavior you want at the bedside. Distrust tools that always answer everything.
4. **Hold conflicting sources open.** When references disagree, the honest state is "sources conflict," not whichever the AI ranked first. Bring the conflict to a human who can judge it.
5. **Escalate by stakes.** For a flashcard definition, a quick check suffices. For anything that could reach a patient, a grade, or another person, full verification — and at the bedside, the patient and the monitor outrank every summary.

## The trap this guards against

Substitution: AI output quietly becoming the conclusion instead of an input to yours. It arrives gradually — the summary you stopped checking, the "prepared protocol" you started trusting, the proactive alert you acted on before verifying. The hypothesis frame is the antidote: useful exactly because you still do the judging.

## In your library

This discipline is why governed knowledge tools carry citations. A retrieval system worth trusting shows you **which source, which version, and its review state** — and refuses to answer without an indexed source rather than improvising: no citation, no claim. When you evaluate any AI study tool, that is the bar.
